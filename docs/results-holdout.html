The exam: does it catch skipped steps in 6 videos it had never seen?

I took 6 openly licensed how-to videos of hands-on jobs and planted 52 mistakes in copies of them with code (a step cut out, or two steps swapped), plus one untouched copy of each. The videos were picked at random before I downloaded them, were never used while I built the checker, and every copy was checked once. The record (which videos, when, what it cost) is in bench/holdout.json; checker version v2.

What this page answers. If someone skips a step in a hands-on job, or does two steps in the wrong order, and it is on camera, does watchverify notice? (watchverify runs on Muse Spark, Meta's AI that understands video.) And does it leave good work alone?

How to read it. Think of a spell-checker. A red mark is a firm call: "this step is missing" or "out of order". A yellow mark means "a person should look at this moment". No mark means it passed. A useful spell-checker underlines most typos and few correct words.

What happened (I made the mistakes with code, so the right answer was known before it looked):

  • Good: a step cut out (26 tests): it marked the right step 21 times, 8 red ("missing") and 13 yellow. The other 5 were not called missing: 1 got a red mark for the wrong reason (out of order), 2 got yellows saying "done another way", and 2 got no mark at all.
  • Good: a job done right (6 recordings): 5 came back with no marks at all; 1 got a mark for nothing.
  • Bad: false alarms: on steps nobody touched it put a yellow mark on about 1 in 7 (32 of 227) and a red one on 5. So yellow is a hint, not proof: even a checker that noticed nothing would have marked about 4 of the 26 cut steps yellow. It marked 13.
  • Bad: two steps swapped (19 tests): 7 red ("out of order"). 6 more got yellow (32%), but by the same arithmetic (about 1 in 7 fine steps gets one) a pair of untouched steps gets a yellow about 26% of the time, so those barely count; 1 more got red marks for the wrong reason (both steps called missing).
  • Fine print: two backup rules make many of the yellow marks. One uses SAM 3.1, Meta's object tracker, to measure how long the tool is in use at each step; the other marks a step much shorter than the expert's. With both off, 13 of 26 cut steps are not counted as noticed, instead of 5.

What it would mean for a supervisor. Instead of watching every recording, you open the ones with marks and jump to the marked moments: about 2 per broken recording here, 0.3 per good one. Doing that on these videos, 21 of the 26 cut steps would have been put in front of you as missing or worth a look, 3 more with a mark for another reason, and 2 with no mark at all. That only helps if you can tell from the marked moment that the step is missing. It is a pointer for a person, not an inspector that signs off a job.

What these numbers can't tell you. Each test clip is 14 to 60 seconds with 1 to 8 steps; a real job can run an hour. The mistakes were made by cutting video, which is not quite how people skip steps (see the README). The untouched copies are the expert's own video, re-cut, which is easier to pass clean than a different person doing the job. It can't yet tell one bolt from another. And 26 tests is a small number: read every figure as rough. What it adds and what it's good for: the README (EXTERNAL · source repo ↗).

Where good and bad sit (rough lines, argue with them): good if at least 75% of cut steps get a mark, at least 50% of swaps get red, at least 80% of good recordings come back clean, and at most 10% of untouched steps get yellow.

What these numbers measure. The cut points, and which planted mistakes are graded, come from labels a person confirmed before any check, not from the model under test. The step list itself is still Muse Spark's: every rate is of the steps the model listed. Two rules that send steps to a person read model-made seconds (SAM timing, and the cut-short rule on the model's own seconds); the table below gives the numbers with and without each.
How the test works. For each video, Muse Spark watched a 30 to 75 second stretch once and wrote the step-by-step procedure (the reference). Then code cut new versions of the clip with one mistake each: one step removed, or two neighboring steps swapped. Because code made the cuts, the planted mistake is known for every version, like an answer key written before the exam. Each version was then checked against the reference without the checker knowing which mistake it held. Caught: the checker named the planted mistake and failed the clip. Flagged: it sent the planted step to a person (can't tell, cut short, or its two sensors disagreed). Missed: the planted step looked fine to it. Not graded: left out of the "of N" with its reason and who decided, and still counted in "planted": the person marked the step not visible to a camera or not a real step, or removing it leaves no footage (a clip's only step). Base rate: "flagged" counts only mean something beside how often untouched steps are flagged too; the README gives both per step. The SAM order rule is off in these numbers (it saw steps in the opposite order on an untouched copy of the expert's own clip) and appears only as a with-it row below.
How the mistakes are cut. A person confirmed, from stills of the expert's clip (human:owner), where each step's hand work starts and ends, whether a camera shows it, and whether it is a real step of the task; a different AI model (not the one being tested) proposed the times from the stills, and the person changed 0 of the 29 steps' proposed values; the labels were committed before any check. Mistakes planted on steps the person marked not visible or not real are not graded (6). Steps the person saw that the model never listed count as planted removals, not graded (0): the checker could never catch them. The model under test still wrote the step list and its descriptions; "step k removed" means the person-confirmed stretch of the model's step k. A removal also drops the in-between stretches on both sides of the step; where the model listed few steps those are long, and 7 of the 26 graded removals took out more than 30% of the clip.

Each rule that rests on another model output, switched off

Five of the checker's rules use a model output other than the check itself. Each may only leave a step as it was or send it to review, never pass a step the check failed or sent to review (a property test checks this on 3,000 random checks for each rule). Sending a failed step to review is still a softening, and the self-check marks also leave mistakes out of the grading. Self-check marks: a step the checker did not find in the expert's own clip is reported "can't tell", never missing, and a mistake planted on it is not graded. Narration-only: a checkpoint that is only heard, never seen, is "can't tell". SAM timing: SAM 3.1 tracks the tool, and a small nearest-neighbor model fitted on the expert's clip says which place belongs to which step; tracked seconds at a step's place can turn "missing" into "can't tell" or send a step to review. Its labels are Spark's step seconds, so it is not independent of the reference, and here every clip is cut from the expert's own footage with the same fixed camera, so SAM is measured in-sample: its help on these clips may not carry over to a new performance. Other moments: the checker lists every other moment it saw a step; two steps out of order that another listed moment would put in order go to review, not fail. Confirm looks: a second look at the stretch where the check placed a step it called seen; "not seen" sends the step to review. Every number, with and without each:

Limits of this test

  • Development set, model-made truth: see the first note. The held-out grading is the test that counts.
  • The cuts follow step boundaries the model drew from stills; where they are off by a frame or two, a removed step can leave a trace behind, and a neighbor can lose an edge (which shows up as an untouched step sent to review).
  • A swapped clip has visible jumps (a cover back on after it came off). A real second performance would not.
  • The correct clips are the expert's own, which is easier than another person doing the task right.
  • About 30 to 45 graded mistakes per table means each rate is uncertain by roughly 15 points either way.
